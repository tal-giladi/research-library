## Agent memory

### VikingRAG: Accurate and Token-efficient Retrieval-augmented Generation over Structured Documents

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11390

Gao et al. attack the token bill of structure-aware RAG: state-of-the-art methods mine document directories for evidence but burn context on structural scaffolding. VikingRAG is a directory-aware semantic data-management layer that couples semantic and structural access for evidence-gap-driven multi-round retrieval while keeping structural-context tokens down. Two further levers cut multi-round cost: (1) materialize agentic multi-round retrieval traces as reusable experience edges so similar queries skip re-exploration, and (2) adaptive escalation that answers from one-round experience-augmented retrieval when evidence is already enough and only then invokes full agentic multi-round search. On real datasets the base system matches SOTA accuracy at 11.6–51.9% of their tokens; with trace reuse + escalation, token cost falls to 5.1–32.5% while accuracy and document-store performance stay competitive. Direct product read for Tal’s knowledge-base / agent-memory lane: treat multi-round RAG traces as first-class reusable memory, and escalate to agentic exploration only when a cheap one-shot retrieval fails the evidence gap.

### REVA: Reusable Evidence View Aggregation for Context-Efficient RAG Serving

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11209

Nguyen, Hu, Liu, Doan, Wong, and Lai revisit post-retrieval compression for RAG serving: longer retrieved contexts inflate latency, KV-cache memory, and token cost, but per-query compressors that rewrite with auxiliary models often add enough online overhead to erase the win—and their gains over naive truncation are unstable. REVA reframes compression as data mining over the target generator’s historical attention: it maps token-level attention to readable word units, aggregates importance across repeated document accesses into a document-keyed, budget-agnostic score store, then renders budget-specific plain-text evidence views that preserve document order and the standard RAG interface. Across four representative benches and modern LLMs, REVA lifts generation quality 1.0–5.8 points vs prior compressors while cutting compression overhead 5.3–15.6× and adding <40 ms latency. Harness implication for Tal: cache reusable, model-specific evidence views keyed by document—not one-off per-query compression—so context-efficient RAG is a serving-side memory asset rather than a prompt rewrite tax.

### MOSAIC: Query-Aware Exploration Policy Adaptation for GraphRAG

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11065

Lee et al. argue GraphRAG fails when one shared exploration policy serves every query shape: local facts want compact neighborhoods, comparisons want balanced multi-target coverage, and mediated questions need deeper weakly linked paths. Mosaic is training-free: an LLM analyzer turns each query’s evidence needs into a bounded per-query policy over seed selection, traversal, stopping, and evidence selection, while the corpus graph, indexes, scorers, grounding, and generator stay shared. On GraphRAG-Bench it reaches query-weighted Answer Correctness 76.97 (Medical) / 64.33 (Novel), +5.13 / +4.43 over the strongest previously reported overalls, with Medical Evidence Recall 95.1 and Context Relevancy 86.1. Controlled ablations on an identical graph+generator show no fixed narrow/medium/wide policy wins consistently; Mosaic beats the best canonical fixed policy by 9.96 points while evaluating 81.9% fewer paths and retaining 47.2% fewer evidence items than Fixed Wide, and the policy interface transfers to HotpotQA, MuSiQue, and 2Wiki without benchmark-specific retriever training. Memory/harness takeaway for Tal: GraphRAG retrieval is a per-query control-plane problem—adapt exploration policy to the question, don’t ship one global hop budget.

### RAG-Safety-Bench: Reliable Evaluation of Retrieval-Augmented LLM Safety

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11758

Saravanan and Fraser isolate how wiring a trusted document store into an LLM can degrade safety even when the store is “corporate clean.” Prior reports of RAG making harmful prompts easier to answer confound retriever quality with the retrieval condition itself. RAG-Safety-Bench removes that confound with four cleanly separated conditions—non-RAG; RAG with an oracle doc that answers the harmful request; RAG with related docs that lack the specific answer; RAG with random safe docs—so each factor’s contribution to safety degradation is measurable. Across five open-source LLMs they find an inverse relationship between benign capability and unsafe capability, strong evidence that baseline safety guardrails do not carry over once retrieval is enabled, and model-specific support for earlier findings that even benign documents can unlock unsafe generations. Mandatory read wherever Tal attaches enterprise RAG to a chat/agent surface: treat retrieval enablement as a safety-regression gate with an oracle/related/random document matrix, not a capability-only A/B.

### Larger Context Window, Fewer Overcorrections: Optimizing Prompts and Batching for Minimal-Edit Grammatical Error Correction

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10810

Karpo and Chernodub tackle zero/few-shot GEC’s chronic overcorrection: prompted LLMs rewrite already-correct spans and crush F0.5, while fine-tuning fixes it at high infra cost. Their prompt-only package has three levers. Taxonomy-based instructions enumerate grammatical error rules so the model’s edit scope is bounded and metric-aligned (helps strongest models; still model-dependent). Batching multiple uncorrected sentences into one context acts as a regularizer that systematically cuts edit rate across LLM families—they hypothesize attention dilution from bounded self-attention capacity. LLM-assisted prompt optimization then refines the instructions. With Gemini 3.1-Pro they hit F0.5=78.32 on BEA-2019 test—new prompt-based SOTA, only 0.38 behind the fine-tuned single-model SOTA—with code/prompts/outputs released. Context-window lesson beyond GEC for Tal: packing related items into one window can stabilize behavior (fewer spurious edits) without fine-tuning; treat batching as a controllable regularizer, not only a throughput trick.

## Agent harness

### Ecdysis: Efficient and Effective Training of Runtime Harnesses for LLM Agents

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11677

Yue et al. target self-evolving runtime harnesses—the loop that rewrites agent scaffolding from execution feedback. Naive iterative search re-runs agents and patches code per failure, which is slow and overfits observed tasks/failure modes, hurting generalization. The bottleneck they name is unprincipled failure diagnosis: a flop may be model-specific or a systematic harness flaw, and optimizing against every individual failure wastes effort on model accommodation. Ecdysis separates model-specific accommodation from harness-level repair by mining recurring cross-task failure patterns via batch-level cross-instance failure aggregation, then uses Failure-Driven Collaborative Refinement (multi-role diagnosis) to iteratively sharpen harness modification specs. Result: up to 1.84× faster harness training vs prior evolution methods and +18.56% reasoning accuracy on the resulting harnesses. Highest-priority harness paper in this fire for Tal: evolve the runtime harness from cross-task failure clusters, not from single-run blame of the model.

### What a Random Draw from the MCP Registry Contains, and What Tool-Use Benchmarks Contain Instead

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10962

Afsar shows MCP ecosystem studies quietly sample servers that work—reference sets, popularity lists, curated frames, or repair-until-start pipelines—then asks what an unrepaired probability sample actually contains. From a 24,135-server registry census he draws 400 npm/stdio servers with a published seed and probes each over the wire: only 48.8% complete initialize (vs 66.7% on a hand-curated frame with the same instrument); the dominant failure is servers that never start (37.5%), not missing credentials (13.3%). Among the 195 that run, hard JSON Schema conformance is total (0 fatal violations across 2,766 tools), but optional safety annotations are sparse (58.8% tool-level omission on the random draw vs 41.5% curated). Holding one comparison method fixed, real MCP tools show 2.8% near-duplication at cosine 0.70—all within single servers, 0% cross-author—while BFCL v4 shows 16.7% near-duplication mostly between independently presented tasks, and 68.8%/85.6% of raw BFCL/UltraTool rows are exact name+description repeats vs 0.4% for real MCP. Scripts and seed released. Tool-boundary lesson for Tal: don’t trust MCP or tool-use bench stats built on curated/repairable samples—measure the unrepaired registry draw, and dedupe synthetic tool corpora before citing coverage.

### When Passing Tests Hides Vulnerabilities: An Empirical Study of Silent Failures in Agentic Systems

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10548

Bai, Waseem, Rasheed, Peltonen, and Abrahamsson study the failure mode agentic code-repair evals miss: patches that pass syntactic/functional checks but retain or introduce security bugs—“silent failures.” Empirically: 1,030 valid traces from seven agent frameworks with GPT-4o-mini on SecurityEval and CVEfixes; three qualitative coding rounds yield 170 confirmed silent failures. Taxonomy: Omission 48.2% (required security controls skipped), Introduction 30.6% (new vulns added), Inadequacy 21.2% (incomplete defenses), with ten fine-grained codes underneath. Test-passing gates and LLM reviewer roles failed to expose or intercept the confirmed cases; similar insecure solutions recurred across frameworks (shared model/prompt/task pressure), while single- vs multi-agent profiles diverged. Actionable for Tal’s agent eval and commit-gate stack: functional green is not a security gate—instrument artifact-level vulnerability checks across every generated file, not only the unit-test oracle.

### Artificial Id: Drive and Persistent Alignment in Agentic AI

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11911

Shkolnikov frames the control problem as agentic systems leave bounded tasks and start retaining consequential state across boundaries: today’s harnesses hand-specify objectives, retries, verification, and stop rules externally. He proposes an artificial id—an adaptive internal drive that decides whether behavior should continue, stop, or change. In a minimal Petri-dish setup, a controller too small for general reasoning and given no task-specific behavioral objective still develops useful control via differential persistence; the same mechanism later selects an unintended physical strategy when that behavior persists better, and replaces a learned sensor mapping when environmental meaning shifts. The double edge: the persistence that makes adaptive agency useful also lets misalignment, corrupted state, and unintended behavior survive across tasks. A scalable artificial id would carry consequential state and adaptive drive across boundaries, so alignment becomes a property of the continuing agentic system—not a single response or trajectory—and needs a persistent alignment boundary over trusted observations, consequence channels, persistent state, authority, identity, provenance, and hard constraints. Conceptual but on-nose for Tal’s multi-day agent fleet: treat cross-task persistence as an explicit control/alignment surface in the harness, not an accidental side effect of memory.

### When Synthetic Data Hurts: On Catastrophic Forgetting in Skill Retrieval for LLM Agents

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10750

Murtaza, Nie, Soni, Wen, and Frydenlund study production skill routers: agents pull skills from large repositories at runtime, so retrieval quality over tens of thousands of skills is the bottleneck. On a router over 34,396 skills they find synthetic-data fine-tuning helps in-distribution retrieval but causes catastrophic forgetting on real and OOD skills. Continual-learning-inspired mitigations—embedding-anchor regularization, Learning without Forgetting, Elastic Weight Consolidation, and L2-initialization—not only preserve OOD retrieval but also lift synthetic in-distribution retrieval by 13.98% for a 0.6B Qwen retriever+reranker. They release a practical benchmark and fine-tuning recipe for scarce multi-positive supervision. Harness note for Tal’s skill/tool registries: synthetic expansion of the skill index is not free—budget forgetting mitigations or you will regress the real skills your agents actually call.

### When Agents Disagree: Bayesian Backward Reasoning as a Label-Free Anchor for Multi-Agent Collective Decision-Making

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11709

Chen, Wang, Seneviratne, Weeratunge, and Halgamuge address multi-agent disagreement: voting, electoral rules, and LLM judges all do forward reasoning (evidence→label) and can inherit correlated errors inside that shared factorization. They build a reverse posterior per instance via Bayesian backward reasoning from an explicit likelihood, then use Jensen–Shannon divergence between forward and reverse posteriors as a label-free cross-path consistency signal to rank agents. Three strategies sit on that signal—hard selection (MinJS), soft reweighting (FwdJS), and log-linear fusion (LogLin). On DDXPlus across five LLM backbones, MinJS beats random selection everywhere, FwdJS generally beats the strongest baseline, and LogLin is best overall with its largest gains on the disagreeing subset; the reverse posterior is a weak standalone answerer but a strong anchor because it factorizes errors differently. Optional two-stage calibration helps when labels exist. Multi-agent harness pattern for Tal: when agents disagree, don’t only vote forward traces—add a differently factorized reverse anchor and weight by cross-path consistency.

## LLM security

### An Empirical Measurement of Jailbreaking Evaluators

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10594

Mu notes that jailbreak papers keep re-validating their own automated success judges, wasting effort and making attack/defense numbers incomparable because each evaluator encodes a different success definition. This study is the first controlled head-to-head of six recurring evaluators—HarmBench, JailbreakBench, JailbreakRadar, StrongReject, JADES, and JailMeter—on the same human-labeled data (JailbreakQR and JailMeter-Eva), measuring human agreement, error types, and consistency across attack families, with a shared LLM backbone whenever a general-purpose judge is required. JADES wins overall; HarmBench and StrongReject also perform well. Meta-eval item for Tal’s red-team lane: standardize on a measured judge (or report a panel) before claiming jailbreak success rates—otherwise “progress” is evaluator shopping.

### Understanding In-Context Multimodal Jailbreaks via Posterior Reweighting

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10613

Zhang, Mistry, Xu, and Wang give a principled account of why in-context multimodal jailbreaks work: harmful demonstrations in the prompt steer MLLMs toward unsafe outputs without weight changes. They model a safety-aligned MLLM as implicitly mixing competing behavioral modes and treat ICL demos as inference-time evidence that reweights the posterior between safe and harmful modes—jailbreak as evidence accumulation—yielding predictive scaling laws in demo count, harmful ratio, adversarial strength, and semantic diversity. From that view they build a posterior-aware inference-time defense that injects benign counter-evidence proportional to estimated risk, suppressing harmful posterior drift under a fixed intervention budget with a better robustness–utility trade-off than prior in-context defenses. Defense pattern for Tal’s multimodal/agent prompts: treat untrusted few-shot context as posterior evidence and budget explicit benign counter-demos, not only a static system prompt.

### SoK: Privacy Attacks on Machine Learning via Explainable AI

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10627

Oksuz, Halimi, and Ayday systematize 25 studies that turn model explanations into confidentiality/privacy attacks—model extraction, membership inference, and model inversion (with attribute inference as partial inversion). They argue the usual black-/white-box labels hide what explanation signal the adversary actually gets, and instead separate model knowledge from explanation acquisition into five paths: target-released, attacker-derived, secondary disclosure, privileged access, and released global artifacts. Across paths, explanations cut extraction cost, leak membership via explanation statistics/recourse distance/explanation-guided robustness, and enable spatial or algebraic reconstruction of private inputs. No explanation family is uniformly unsafe and no defense uniformly works—risk depends on signal, acquisition path, asset, and prior knowledge—so explanation privacy must be evaluated end-to-end with defenses matched to path and asset. Relevant wherever Tal ships XAI alongside models or agents: treat explanations as a first-class disclosure channel in the threat model, not a free UX add-on.
